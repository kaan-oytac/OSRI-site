Secondary school research in the Okanagan Valley

Research reviewed and published by students in the Okanagan.

OSRI suggests research questions, allows students to suggest their own research question for others to answer, reviews student manuscripts against a published ethics standard, and hosts the finished work online. Open to students at any secondary school from Osoyoos to Enderby, at no cost to authors.

About OSRI

Student-led research in the Okanagan is rare, and the ethics approval that formal research requires is out of reach for most high school students. OSRI exists to close that gap responsibly.

Ethics notice

OSRI ethics review is not IRB or REB approval and does not substitute for it. It certifies that a study met OSRI's published standard, which follows the Regeneron ISEF rules for human participant research.

  1. Anthropomorphism of AI and Use Intensity as Mediating Variables in Adolescents’ Expectations for Human Relationships

    Kaan Martin Oytac · Okanagan Mission Secondary (OKM) · Grade 11 · 27 September 2026

    Adolescence is a developmental period in which internal working models of relationships are actively revised, making teens disproportionately sensitive to the relational environments they inhabit. AI companions, which are much more available, consistently validating, and conflict-avoidant than humans, now make up a significant portion of adolescents’ social life. This paper asks and contributes to answering the question of whether AI use predicts shifts in adolescents’ expectations from their other relationships across four dimensions—availability, emotional validation, conflict tolerance, and honesty. Furthermore, the paper seeks to determine whether this observation is influenced by the anthropomorphization of AIs. By investigating attachment theory, research on AI-mediated social cognition, literature on chatbots and human relationships, and a Canadian high school’s student population via a survey, this cross-sectional mixed-methods study (1) formulates five hypotheses (H1-H5) and (2) creates its own measure of AI anthropomorphism by extending the RoPE scale. It then (3) tests hypotheses among a student body (N = 23) and (4) builds plausible explanations behind important findings. Results show a striking split between users and non-users: users rated their expectations for AI’s availability indistinguishably from family members, while non-users rated it the lowest out of any counterpart. Additionally, users' expectations of AI were considerably higher than non-users. Despite significant changes in expectations for AI, however, there was only a negligible change with expectations for human counterparts between users and non-users. These findings may help inform digital literacy curricula, parental guidance around AI use, and the ethical design of conversational AI systems accessible to adolescents.

    Reviewed and deemed ready for publication by Dr. Jonthon Coulson. The author is OSRI's Founding Editor and took no part in the decision on this paper.
